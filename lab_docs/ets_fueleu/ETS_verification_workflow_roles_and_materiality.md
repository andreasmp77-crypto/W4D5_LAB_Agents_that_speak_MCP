# EU ETS & MRV Maritime – Verification Workflow, Roles and Materiality

This document provides a practical overview of how verification works under MRV Maritime and EU ETS for shipping, which parties are involved in the compliance chain, and how concepts like reasonable assurance and materiality are used in practice.

It is aimed at operational and compliance teams, and at AI agents that need to reason about verification without replacing professional judgement.

## Roles and Responsibilities in the Compliance Chain

Under MRV Maritime and EU ETS, several key actors interact in a structured compliance cycle:

- **Shipping company**
  - Develops and maintains ship-level monitoring plans (MPs).
  - Monitors fuel consumption, voyage data and emissions throughout the year.
  - Prepares ship-level emissions reports and, where in ETS scope, company-level emissions reports.
  - Submits reports for verification and then to the administering authority.

- **Verifier**
  - Independent legal entity accredited to perform verification.
  - Assesses monitoring plans, the way MPs are implemented, and the data sources that support emissions reports.
  - Checks that reported data are complete, consistent, accurate and in line with MRV and ETS requirements.
  - Issues verification reports and opinions (e.g. “verified as satisfactory”) and, for ship-level reports, documents of compliance.

- **Administering Authority (AA)**
  - Approves monitoring plans where required.
  - Receives verified emissions reports and verification reports.
  - Enforces compliance, including conservative estimation of emissions if reports are not satisfactory or not submitted on time.
  - May perform additional checks and spot inspections.

- **National Accreditation Body (NAB)**
  - Accredits verifiers according to relevant accreditation regulations and standards.
  - Monitors verifiers’ competence and performance through surveillance and reassessment.
  - Cooperates with other bodies to maintain confidence in verification activities.

These roles together form a chain that goes from drafting monitoring plans, through monitoring and reporting, to verification, approval, and enforcement.

## Key Verification Principles

Verification is not just about checking one number; it is about assessing whether the entire reporting system produces reliable, faithful emissions data. Fundamental principles include:

- **Completeness**
  - All relevant voyages, emission sources and required data fields are included.
  - No gaps or omissions that could materially change reported totals.

- **Consistency and comparability**
  - Data and methods are applied consistently over time, allowing comparison across reporting years.
  - Changes are documented and justified.

- **Accuracy and integrity of methodology**
  - Emissions are calculated correctly based on sound data and recognised methods.
  - Underlying measurement and data collection processes are robust.

- **Transparency and continuous improvement**
  - Data and methods are documented clearly so they can be understood and checked.
  - Issues and recommendations identified by verifiers are addressed over time to strengthen the system.

These principles underpin the verifier’s opinion and directly influence whether misstatements or non-conformities are considered material.

## Reasonable Assurance vs Limited Assurance

In assurance engagements, two broad levels of assurance are often distinguished:

- **Reasonable assurance**
  - High but not absolute level of confidence that the emissions report is free from material misstatements.
  - Requires the verifier to perform sufficient, detailed work: understanding data flows, testing controls, sampling data, and verifying back to primary sources where necessary.
  - MRV Maritime requires verification for EU ETS to be carried out at a reasonable level of assurance.

- **Limited assurance**
  - Moderate level of confidence, with less depth and breadth of verification.
  - Verification activities may be more limited, increasing the risk that misstatements remain undetected.
  - This level is generally not sufficient to meet MRV Maritime requirements for ETS verification.

In practice, reasonable assurance means that the verifier must obtain enough evidence to support a positive opinion, taking into account materiality thresholds and verification risk.

## Materiality and Types of Findings

**Materiality** is a key concept for verification. It helps determine:

- How deeply the verifier needs to test and investigate potential issues.
- Whether a misstatement or non-conformity is serious enough to affect the opinion.

Important points about materiality:

- Every identified misstatement or non-conformity should, in principle, be corrected if possible.
- Materiality is not a “tolerance band” that allows errors; it is a tool for judging whether remaining issues are serious enough to impact the verification opinion.
- Only reports free from material misstatements can be verified as satisfactory.

In verification reports and templates, findings are often classified as:

- **Uncorrected misstatements**
  - Differences between reported data and what should have been reported.
  - Can be over-stated or under-stated values, or incorrect application of methodologies.
  - May be material or non-material.

- **Uncorrected non-conformities**
  - Failures to comply with specific MRV or ETS requirements.
  - May include missing monitoring plan approvals, incomplete procedures, or lack of required evidence.

- **Recommendations for improvement**
  - Suggestions to strengthen monitoring, reporting and control systems.
  - Do not necessarily prevent a “verified as satisfactory” opinion if core requirements are met.

Understanding these categories helps interpret verification reports and supports the design of internal controls and AI assistants that flag potential compliance risks.
